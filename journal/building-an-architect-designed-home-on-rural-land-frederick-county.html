Building an architect-designed home on rural land in Frederick County

A good piece of land in the Middletown Valley or the hills around Braddock Heights is a big part of why people build here. It is also where most of the early surprises come from. Before anyone frames a wall, the land has to be understood, tested and prepared, and those decisions shape the house as much as the drawings do.

Here is how we think about it.

Start with the site, not the house

On a city lot the house usually decides the site. On acreage it is the other way around. Where the house sits depends on the slope, the views, the sun, the soil, where the septic field can go and where the well can be drilled. A spot that looks perfect on a walk may not pass a perc test, or may need a long and expensive driveway.

That's why we like to walk a property with the owner and the architect early, sometimes before the design is very far along. A builder who plans the site work early can tell you fairly quickly what a location will cost to reach, clear and dig.

Septic comes first

Most rural lots in Frederick County are on septic, and the septic decides more than people expect. The perc test sets where the field can go, and that space, plus a reserve area, is space the house, the driveway and the pool can't use. On some lots it decides the whole layout.

Get the perc testing done and the field located before the design is locked. Otherwise the architect may end up redrawing a house around a field that can only go in one place.

The well

The well is less predictable. You don't really know the depth or flow until it's drilled, and the county has rules on how far it must be from the septic system and the house. We plan for a range, not a single number, and we work out where the water comes into the house and where the equipment goes with the architect and the mechanical engineer. In a modern house with a clean mechanical room, that matters.

Grading and steep lots

Much of the best land here isn't flat. A house that steps down a hillside or sits on a ridge can be beautiful, and it can also mean retaining walls, rock, drainage that has to be worked out carefully, and a foundation that does more work than usual.

We plan and price the lot clearing, rough grading and foundation excavation in preconstruction, and we schedule them around the foundation, which matters when the rest of the schedule depends on getting the foundation in.

Grading is also design. How the ground meets the house, where water goes in a storm and how the driveway approaches all shape how a modern house looks when it's done. We would rather work those out with the architect on paper than in the field.

Concrete that shows

Many architect-designed homes on rural land use the foundation as part of the architecture: a board-formed concrete wall coming out of the hill, or a base the house sits on. When the concrete is the finished surface, the formwork has to be treated like finish carpentry. We learned that building Blue Ridge House for Olson Kundig on a knoll in Delaplane, Virginia, where the board-formed concrete base is one of the main features of the house. The same care applies to a hillside house in the valley.

Planning for a second building

Many owners on acreage also want a pool house, guest suite or studio, now or later. It is worth siting it at the same time as the main house, because it may need its own septic and well connections, and it changes the grading and the driveway. We recently built a pool house ADU in Alexandria, VA, designed by MODE4 Architecture, and the guest house at Blue Ridge House for Olson Kundig, where a rolling wall with a Murphy bed on each side lets one room change use. On both, the site planning mattered as much as it did for the main house.

Working with the architect

The best rural projects we've built have one thing in common: the builder was involved early. The architect knows the design. We know what the land will cost and how the site work fits the schedule. Putting both together in preconstruction, before the drawings are finished, saves the owner from the worst kind of surprise, finding out after the design is done that the site can't support it at the budget.

It also protects the design. When the site costs are known early, there's money left for the details that make the house what it is, instead of value engineering them out at the end. We build what you draw, and that's easier when the ground under it has been planned as carefully as the house.
